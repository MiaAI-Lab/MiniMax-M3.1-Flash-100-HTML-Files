寂

There is a word in Japanese for the way an object, once repaired, becomes more beautiful than it was new. The repair is not hidden. The crack is gold.

円相 — the ensō, drawn in one breath

三つの石

A zen garden is not a picture of nature. It is an argument about attention: gravel raked into water, three stones placed by hand, and the whole arrangement disturbed by a single visitor's footstep — which is the point.

常有

The ordinary. Nothing here is remarkable, and that is why it is restful.

不完

Imperfection. The chipped edge, the unfinished corner, the thing that is nearly right.

間

Emptiness. The space that the objects do not fill is part of the composition.

朴

Simplicity is not the absence of detail. It is detail with nothing to prove. A bowl of rice, a stone, a line of ink that dries where it wants to.

"The pine wants to be a pine. The crane wants to be a crane. The mountain wants to be a mountain."
— a ninth-century proverb, roughly

侘び — wabi

Beauty that is lonely, weathered, and short. A single flower in an empty vase. The pleasure lies in knowing it will be gone in three days, and in choosing it anyway.

寂び — sabi

Beauty in the process of decay. Patina, moss, the softening of a sharp edge. Not repair, exactly, but the slow work of time understood as a collaborator rather than an enemy.

Nothing on this page is new. The paper is a photograph of fibre, the circle was drawn by hand in a single movement, and the stones have been in the same garden since 1974. What is new is the arrangement — and even that, only slightly.
