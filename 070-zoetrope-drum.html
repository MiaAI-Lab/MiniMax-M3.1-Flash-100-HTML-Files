A zoetrope is a drum with a ring of pictures around the inside and a slit at the top. Spin it, look through the slit, and the pictures do not flicker — they run. The eye cannot resolve a change faster than about fifty times a second, so if the drum passes the slit often enough, the separate pictures are stitched into motion.

The zoetrope is attributed to William George Horner, a Bristol engraver, who published it in 1834 as a "daedaleum" — a mechanical toy that showed the illusion of motion. Three years later the Parisian optician SimonStampfer patented a version with a slitted cardboard drum, and it was a triumph at the Paris industrial exhibition of 1839.

The later phenakistiscope, spinning disc with painted figures seen through a hole, is the same idea flattened. And in 1889 Étienne-Jules Marey and Georges Eastman were both independently making the leap from the spinning drum to the strip of film.

Conditions

  1. Enough pictures. Below about ten the motion jerks and the eye catches the gaps.
  2. A narrow slit. Wide slit, wide flicker. One slit is the classic arrangement.
  3. Enough speed. The drum must pass the slit faster than the eye can follow separately.
  4. Even light. A stuttering lamp in a dark room ruins it faster than anything.
  5. Consistent spacing. Uneven picture spacing shows up as a hitch in the gait.
